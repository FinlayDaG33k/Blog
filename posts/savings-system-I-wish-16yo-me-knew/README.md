## Learning to save as a kid.

I, like many Dutch children, would get a small amount of pocket money from his parents.    
At first I got 10 cents, then later I got 20 cents, then 50 cents and by the age of 5, I got a whopping 1 euro each week!  
Just to then immediately head over to the candystore and spend it all on candy...
Mind you, we're talking about the early-2000's where 1 euro could get you a fairly decent amount of candy.  
The same happened when I got a bit older, except instead of candy, I would spend it on toys.  

My parents weren't all that rich (we weren't poor and had a fairly comfortable life but still had to turn a dime every now and then), so always getting the latest and greatest of things wasn't in it.  
While I was at one of my neighbour's place after school (because mom had a meeting at work and dad was a train driver), I was playing on this weird little thing their son had.  
And while I can no longer remember what game I was playing, I was fascinated by this little device we now know as the "Nintendo DS".  
I wanted one myself... But by that age I got "only" 2 euro a week, which I spent on other toys too...  
My parents told me about this mind boggling concept for me: Saving.  

Instead of immediately spending my money, I could keep it in my little "elephant" (literally a piggy bank but shaped like an elephant).  
This concept opened a whole new world for me, so I started saving up...  
My weekly pocket money and when I got my school report, I would save up all the money I got from that too.  
When I got my summer report in 2005, we went to grandma... Whom gave me 50 euro's.  
So after we left grandma's place, we went to the nearest toystore and I immediately went looking for a Nintendo DS.  
Once I found it and saw the pricetag (if memory serves right, 150 euro), my parents and I counted my money.  
This went a lot easier than expected because (for a reason I cannot remember), I would often exchange 5x2euro coins for a 10 euro bill with my parents.  
I then took it to the check-out, and after they confirmed that it was indeed enough, it was mine!  
Did I have any games for it? Not really, outside my old GBA and GBC games but I didn't care, VICTORY WAS MINE!  
This Nintendo DS, would be the first of many things I saved up for as a child.  
From LEGO sets, to games, to model trains and more game consoles (like a PSP), I was rather fascinated by saving.  
Even go so far as literally calling the bank what the current interest rates were (because it said I got like a few cents on the little monthly thing I got in my mailbox each month).  
I didn't quite understand it but ***SAVING***.

Between that time (age 7) and my late-teens (age 19), nothing all that much changed.  
Just the amount of money I had (due to starting a job) and the pricetag on the stuff I wanted.  
Computer parts, cameras, networking equipment, a driver's license etc. etc.  
Occasionally, I would spend some money on something smaller (like a dinner with my gf at the time) but generally, I would just save up until I had enough for something relatively big.  

## An Issue arrives

But that brings me to a big problem.  
Something that I had to deal with and was a huge headache but never really asked my parents about.  
You see, when I finally had enough money to buy the thing I was saving up for, I would immediately but it.  
So, if something else decided to break or I had some unforeseen expense (or me just forgetting I had said expense), I was in a pickle, because I had nothing to catch it with.  
That fancy camera I bought? That was basically all my money.  
By this time, my parents and I had already struck an agreement in which they saved up part of my money, for bigger things and they could steer me a bit.  
But even with this, it sometimes still caused problems.  

Then, at age 21, when I bought my own car (and thus had to pay for my own fuel, road tax, maintenance etc.), this became a bigger problem, as sometimes I would just be completely broke after having paid all my bills.  

## A first attempt

So then, that meant I had to come up with a solution.  
Luckily, this wasn't all that hard.  
At the end of each month, I would just send some money to my savings account.  
By this time, I already started using a bookkeeping app called [HomeBank](https://www.gethomebank.org/en/index.php), an app I still use today and contains almost all transactions since I started using it in 2017.  
This made it easier to keep track of when I had to send it and how much I should have and when my bills were about to come in, however, this wasn't quite enough.  
The issue was that I could still rather freely take money from my savings.  
It was better than the situation before but not quite stable still.

## Attempt Two: Credit Card Boogaloo!

This issue has been on my mind quite often, especially as my bills were due because I didn't always have enough money.  
Luckily, I didn't always have to pay them exactly at the end of each month but it was still very stressful.  

One day, however, I was discussing with someone on Discord about debt.  
I live in a country where Debit cards are the main type of card.  
Debit cards, unlike Credit cards, do not allow you to spend more than you owe.  
However, that person wasn't Dutch, he was American, where Credit cards are the main type of card.  
As a result of this, I started to look into Credit cards.  
Not in term of which card is the best, but more so how they work and differ from Debit cards.
The gist (that is relevant for this story) is very simple: You borrow money to pay someone and at the end of the month, you pay this back with interest.  
I also learned that many, *many* Americans get into a debt-trap because of this interest (as well as overdraft fees and such).

But this put me to think...  
What if, instead of paying that interest and those fees to a bank... I would just pay them to myself?  
And so, after giving it some thought, I introduced a new system for myself, one that I still use today as well.  
It was very loosely based on the credit card system, without a lot of the things that keep you into the debt-trap and works as follows:

- I can *borrow* from my savings, never just "spend my savings".
- When I borrow something from my savings, I must pay it back at the end of the month (or otherwise ASAP), before buying anything fun.
- When I borrow something from my savings, I must pay back an additional fee (I set this to 10 euro fixed).

This solved a few issues:

- Savings become a priority over fun but fun was still allowed.
- My savings shouldn't easily deplete.
- Borrowing wasn't without "punishment".
- Borrowing would lead to more savings (due to the fees).

There were only 2 major downsides:

- Increased complexity in bookkeeping.
- It required discipline (no "I can pretend this didn't happen once").

## Jars, jars and even more jars! (no Java tho)

This system worked "alright" for a small while but again, issues started to show.  
I entered a new relationship, which required me to drive a lot more.  
However, just a few weeks after buying a nice little trailer for my car...

I got a very rough streak that I didn't quite expect (it always happens after buying something new, ofc it does).  

- Needed new glasses.
- Health insurance.
- Car road tax.
- My car needed fuel.
- My car needed an oil change.
- My birthday (which meant dinner with friends and my gf at the time).
  - Not important and I could have cancelled this with full understanding of said friends and my gf.

So this all meant the savings I had, were basically instantly vapourized.  
And due to this, I needed to redesign my system a bit *again*.  
I looked at what went wrong and noticed a few things:

- Roadtax, health insurance and fuel were *common costs*.
- Oil change and new glasses where part of the things I *needed*.
- My birthday dinner was part of the things I *wanted*.

Some people do budget (eg. 50 euro a week on fuel and 20 euro a month on games etc.) but because life is highly unpredictable, I knew this approach wouldn't work for me.  
So I thought to myself: Why not make little jars in which I already pre-save the common costs (so I always have those ready when I need them) and make an additional jar for things I needed?  
That way, I won't have to dig into my "overall pool" as much when I hit a streak of bad luck.  
So, I did exactly that.  
Luckily my bank allows me to have little jars on my savings account to help me out.

- A jar for each of my "common costs" (health insurance and fuel being 2 separate jars).
- A jar for "other things I may need" (like my phone breaking or any other unforeseen expenses).
- A jar for "emergencies" (just for good measure).

This also had a small benefit, in that while the money was waiting, it would get a small amount of interest.  
Not much but all small bits help.  
So that leaves the question, how much should I save?  
For the "common costs", this was quite easy: One payment should suffice.  
For the "needs" jar, I settled on 3 months of income (at the time) and for the "emergency" jar, I made this 6 months.

Additionally, I made 3 more jars:
- One for the borrow fees (I don't really touch this jar - for no particular reason).
- One for "bonusses" (things like vacation money, birthday money, salary corrections, interest payouts etc. - I don't really touch this jar either).
- One for "daily life" (jepp, this is the money for going out, getting snaccs etc.. It's just a separate jar to get more interest on what I do not use + give me some extra thinking time before I make a purchase).

And finally, I have jars for "bigger savings projects", like car upgrades, solar panels for the house, PC upgrades (when prices are acceptable again someday... I hope...) etc.

So my current system works as follows:

- I have many different jars for each of my "common costs".
  - They hold 1 payment each (+ a bit because it allows for the cost to go up a bit as prices change).
  - If I cannot afford it "out of pocket", I can from these jars.
  - Borrowing from these jars incurs no fees.
  - When I get my paycheck, priority goes to refilling these jars.
- I have 1 jar for "emergencies" and 1 jar for "things I need".
  - These are 6 and 3 months of my income (for 2025) respectively.
  - Borrowing from these means I need to pay it back asap + put an additional 10 in the "borrow fees" jar.
  - I borrow from "needs" first, leaving "emergencies" for when the "needs" jar isn't big enough (which means actual emergencies).
- I try to pay for things out of pocket first.
- I still save an amount per month into a savings project.


My reasoning is as follows:

- Keep "common costs" ready so I don't have to worry about these.
  - Keep them separate so I am always ready for each bill.
- Have big jars for emergencies or if (for some reason), I cannot work for ~9 months. 
- If both my "needs" and "emergency jars" aren't enough, I probably have direct problems way bigger than my financial state.
- Bonus: I also get interest on it all.

## Final Thoughts

This system has served me really well since I adopted it in late-2025.  
The biggest issue it has, is that I have now saved so much that I am literally not eligible for govt support (Bijstand/WW) if I become unemployed.  
That and the bookkeeping is a bit more complex (just a few extra accounts and transactions, no huge issue).  
Additionally, interest rates fluctuate and while currently they are still positive, I have not taken into account what I would do if the interest rates became _negative_.  
So let's hope that never happens.

The emergency jar and needs jar were both set to my monthly income in 2025 which wasn't all that high.  
While these jars are definitely nothing to scoff at, I do still need to re-calibrate them for my current income someday.  
Additionally, I do wish to adjust the jars for each of the "common costs" to each hold a longer period of time, say 6 months.  
That way, I could (in theory) be out of a job for 6 months without really affecting my ability to pay my bills.  
This shouldn't be needed in practise but it'd give me even more peace of mind.
